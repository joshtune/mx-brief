details

What it is: A free Claude skill that ports Stanford's STORM research method (peer-reviewed as 25% more organized than the next best) into a multi-agent pipeline that outputs a verified HTML briefing.

How it works:

  • Five-perspective fan-out: Instead of one prompt (which leaves blind spots), STORM spins up five role-played agents — a Practitioner, Academic, Skeptic, Economist, and Historian — each finding holes the others miss.
  • Convergence pass: The agents' findings are merged and the skill surfaces where the perspectives *disagree*.
  • Verification pass: Six more agents fact-check every claim. Sources get tagged confirmed, corrected, or demoted — so V2 is far more trustworthy than the first pass.
  • Ranked output: Final HTML report includes a 60-second summary, key findings ranked by reliability (e.g. "reliability high, 9/10 — supported by Academic + Skeptic, challenged by Practitioner + Economist"), assumptions the briefing rests on, and explicitly named *missing lenses*.
  • Iterate further: When the skill flags a missing 6th lens (e.g. the customer/frontline view that none of the five owner's-chair perspectives covered), you just tell it to spin that lens up and run a V3.
  • Tailorable: Edit the skill to inject your business context/goals so every report is shaped toward what *you* should do differently with the findings.
  • Benchmark: Nate fed both outputs to Codex (a different model) as a blind judge — it picked the STORM HTML briefing over Claude's native deep research on evidence quality.

vs. Claude's native Deep Research:

  • Claude Code's `deep research` command (shipped with dynamic workflows) kicks off ~100+ background agents (103 in his run) but dumped a thin markdown file with only ~2 confirmed sources and a pile of unconfirmed ones + open questions.
  • STORM produces a consistent, source-verified, reliability-ranked HTML report every time — tailored, not a stat brain-dump.

Google's playbook codifies the exact harness-over-model, spec-first thinking you need to level up your Claude agent skills and mx-workflow.

details

What it is: A walkthrough of Google's new 51-page playbook on the AI-driven SDLC, reorganized by Cole into a single concise diagram covering the industry's converging best practices and terminology.

How it works:

  • Model is ~10%, harness is ~90% — the headline claim: the model you obsess over barely matters; the harness (context, tools, validation loops) determines results. Vibe coding doesn't scale, agentic engineering does.
  • AI-driven SDLC — software development life cycle = idea → requirements → design → implementation → test → review → deploy → maintain. Writing code is only the middle slice.
  • Spec quality is the new bottleneck — AI compresses implementation from 1–3 weeks to minutes/hours, but requirements-gathering (front) and validation (end) stay human-driven and barely get faster. That's why engineers 10x output but businesses don't.
  • AI coding is a spectrum, not a switch — not binary vibe-coding vs. agentic-engineering. The spectrum runs:
  • *Vibe coding* — prompt with no planning, validation = "does it seem to work?"
  • *Structured AI-assisted* — detailed prompts, more deliberate spot-checking
  • *Agentic engineering* — full harness with the agent iterating against its own tests and evals
  • Cole's take — next wave of $1B+ companies will be platforms that speed up requirements-gathering and validation, since the middle (implementation) is largely solved.
  • Why teach it — turning hard-won instincts into precise mental models, diagrams, and shared terminology is the point of the playbook (and this video).

Tools & links:

  • Google's AI-driven SDLC playbook (51-page PDF) — linked in the video description; the source artifact being summarized
  • Cole Medin's channel — ongoing agentic engineering tutorials
  • Archon — Cole's open-source agent orchestration / harness tool (referenced as his broader body of work)
  • Note: this transcript is truncated mid-sentence; full tool list lives in the video description

How Warren Buffett saved Goldman with one investment

37.7K

Buffett's 2008 Goldman bet was confidence, not cash.

A masterclass in how a credible name—not money—can rescue a business when market confidence collapses.

details

What it is: The inside story of Warren Buffett's $5B investment in Goldman Sachs during the 2008 financial crisis—and why it mattered far more as a signal than as capital.

The deal:

  • Berkshire Hathaway invested $5 billion in Goldman Sachs at the peak of the 2008 crisis
  • Buffett framed it purely as a good investment, not a bailout or favor
  • His line on the risk: $5B going bad "is not even a bad hurricane on the East Coast"—signaling the sum was trivial relative to Berkshire's balance sheet

Why it actually worked (the real lever):

  • Goldman had the money already—capital was not the problem
  • What they lacked was the confidence of the world: peers were failing or in distress, and markets couldn't tell Goldman apart from them
  • Perception was the threat—"if you just assert [distress], that scares people"
  • Buffett's name was a public stamp of solvency: if the world's most respected investor buys in, the market re-rates your survival odds

The business lesson:

  • In a panic, credibility is the scarce asset, not cash
  • A trusted third party vouching for you can break a confidence death-spiral
  • Optics and signaling can be worth more than the dollars on the term sheet

Why it matters for you: Reputation and third-party credibility are balance-sheet assets—when trust evaporates, the right name attached to your company can be worth more than the capital it brings.
