Everything Goldman Sachs Taught Me About AI (In 10 minutes)

V — Verify the output:

  • The core distinction, credited to Goldman CIO Marco Argenti: a model's *reasoning* can be useful even when its *final answer* is wrong. Mine the breakdown, verify the result.
  • Verification starts upstream, at the data. Every BI analyst in Nate's role had to pass an internal "data school" — how data gets pulled, cleaned, updated, and turned into something decision-grade.
  • The punchline on data quality: bad data plugged into AI "just gives you the wrong answer faster." Your proprietary data (customers, sales history, internal processes, support tickets) is the moat — but only if it's clean.
  • Verification prompt he actually uses: *"Before you give me the final answer, recheck every number and factual claim. Cite the source for each one, and flag anything you're not fully 100% confident about."*
  • Spot-check triage: don't verify every word. In a report, usually only 2–3 numbers could actually change the decision. Force citations on those, check those yourself.
  • Escalation for high stakes: spin up a separate AI reviewer — or a team of reviewer agents — that check the first output and let the system iterate *before* a human sees anything. But: important claims still need original sources, deterministic tests, or human review. AI review is an additional layer, not the last one.
  • Explicitly framed as tool-agnostic — applies whether you're on Claude Code, Codex, or whatever ships next month.

A — Augment, don't replace:

  • Nate's actual Goldman job was building automations, reports, dashboards, and internal efficiency systems — none of it branded as an "AI initiative." Goldman had been running automated data and risk systems for decades.
  • The takeaway: there was already a huge backlog of problems worth solving *before* agents existed. Hours-long manual reports, data shuffled between systems, teams blocked waiting on other teams, fat-fingered entries, skipped steps, two people running the same process two different ways.
  • The target isn't replacing the human — it's the inconsistency. Humans get tired, miss steps, drift from the process. That's the surface area automation should attack first.

The core claim:

  • Everything else — CLAUDE.md rules, skills, sub-agent instructions — is *guidance*. LLMs are probabilistic; they interpret and drop steps, especially in large codebases with long checklists.
  • Hooks are the only layer that gives you a guarantee. That word is the whole video.
  • He's audited AI coding setups for hundreds of devs and entire companies: almost nobody uses enough hooks.

The five components of a coding harness (his taxonomy):

  • Rules — persistent guidance (CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md)
  • Sub-agents — delegation
  • MCP servers — platform connections
  • Skills — reusable workflows and prompts
  • Hooks — deterministic automations ← the underused one

The canonical example he walks through:

  • Classic failure: your rules say "run the test suite and validate your work." Agent runs *part* of the suite, declares done, half your tests are still red.
  • Hook version: a Stop hook fires when the conversation ends → runs the full suite deterministically → on failure, forces the agent to resume and fix.
  • Same intent, but the agent no longer has to *remember* the order of operations.

The framework — when should a rule become a hook?

  • If it's phrased "when X happens, do Y" → that's event-shaped, make it a hook.
  • If it's a validation/audit/security check → hook, always. Don't ask the model to police itself.
  • If it's logging/observability → hook.
  • Goal: extract the process away from the agent so correctness doesn't depend on instruction-following.

Ownership comes with a whole lotta perks

Buy, borrow, die: how owners legally skip taxes

It explains why owning an appreciating asset beats earning a salary — the core reason a side hustle should end in equity, not just extra income.

details

What it is: A 90-second breakdown of the "buy, borrow, die" strategy — how Elon Musk grows billions in Tesla stock while paying effectively zero income tax, and why the mechanism is available to any owner, not just billionaires.

How it works:

  • Take stock, not salary. Musk draws no cash salary from Tesla. Compensation comes as equity, so there's no W-2 income to tax.
  • Unrealized gains aren't income. If $10B in stock doubles to $20B, the $10B gain is untaxed. The IRS only gets paid on a *sale* — so owners simply don't sell.
  • Borrow against the asset. Need cash to live? Take a securities-backed loan — e.g. $100M borrowed against a $20B position. Loan proceeds aren't income, so they're not taxed. The stock stays put and keeps compounding.
  • Die with the basis step-up. At death, the IRS resets the asset's cost basis to current market value ($20B). The $10B in embedded gains vanishes for tax purposes. The estate settles the loan; the deferred tax bill never arrives.
  • The punchline: this isn't a loophole or a workaround. It's the tax code operating as designed — for people who *own* things rather than people who *earn* wages.

The mechanics behind it:

  • Wage income is taxed the moment it's earned, at the highest ordinary rates. Asset appreciation is taxed only on realization — and possibly never.
  • Debt is not income. That single line is what makes the whole structure work.
  • Step-up in basis (IRC §1014) is the piece that converts "deferred forever" into "forgiven."
  • The scaled-down version: this is exactly why real estate and cash-flowing businesses beat a paycheck. Depreciation, refinancing (cash-out refi is the small-scale securities loan), and holding through death follow the same three-step logic.

Tools & links:

  • No tools, repos, or URLs mentioned in this video — it's a pure concept explainer.

Caveats worth knowing:

  • Securities-backed lines of credit carry margin-call risk if the collateral drops. Concentrated-position holders have been wiped out this way.
  • Estate tax still applies above the exemption threshold; "zero tax" refers to *income* tax on the gains, not the estate.
  • The step-up rule is a live political target and has been proposed for repeal multiple times. Don't build a 30-year plan on a 30-year-old rule surviving unchanged.

Why it matters for you: If your side hustle only ever produces income, you're taxed at the worst possible rate — the goal is to convert that hustle into an owned, appreciating asset you can borrow against instead of sell.
