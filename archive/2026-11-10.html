Five design patterns that make AI-built websites actually convert

Nate gives a concrete, repeatable checklist (copy first, then color discipline) for prompting Claude into sites good enough to sell for $1,000 — though the transcript here covers only the first two patterns and no repos or build walkthrough.

details

What it is: A framework of five patterns Nate says separate AI websites that drive results from generic-looking ones; this transcript covers pattern 1 (mirror test) and the start of pattern 2 (highlighter rule), then cuts off.

How it works:

  • Pattern 1 — The Mirror Test (copy, not design): The right visitor should land on the first screen and feel like they're looking in a mirror: their problem, their world, a clear sign they're in the right place.
  • Why AI fails here: It writes generic copy, so a good-looking site still under-converts.
  • Case study — Truckers Report: Job site where drivers fill one resume and pick from trucking-company offers. Page already converted at ~12.1%. CXL (conversion agency) surveyed drivers and dug into data before touching design.
  • What CXL found: ~half of traffic was on phones (drivers browse on the road), the headline had no benefit, and the hero was a stock handshake photo no driver could relate to.
  • Result: Page rebuilt around the driver converted 79.3% better after a series of tests.
  • Three questions your first screen must answer:
  • Who is this for?
  • What do I get? (outcome, not feature: 'fill your calendar without answering DMs', not 'AI-powered scheduling')
  • What do I do next? (one clear CTA, e.g. 'book your free call')
  • Headline proof points: City Click changed its headline from 'businesses grow faster online' to 'create a web page for your business' and pricing-page click-throughs rose 90%. Campaign Monitor matched one headline word (design/create/build) to what people typed into Google and got 31.4% more signups.
  • Workflow: Answer the three questions yourself, then paste them into Claude *before* asking for any design. This gives much better copy.
  • Tie-in: Same idea as Nate's 'three P's' — pain, person, promise — applied to the first thing someone sees. Think thumbnail/packaging of a YouTube video.
  • Pattern 2 — The Highlighter Rule (color): AI treats color as decoration, picking pleasant colors with weak contrast so nothing stands out.
  • The rule: Pick one standout color and use it only on the thing you want clicked (button, booking link). Highlight everything and nothing is highlighted.
  • Nate's example: First version of the new AI Automation Society site used blue everywhere and looked cheap. He went mostly black and white, kept blue for small key elements, and it felt more premium and clearer.
  • 60-30-10 rule (interior design): Three colors only. Example: pale gray as the main (60%), navy as secondary (30%), burnt orange as accent (10%). Transcript cuts off mid-explanation.
  • Patterns 3–5: Not in the provided transcript, so they are not covered here.
