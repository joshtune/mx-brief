What it is: A grounded explainer on "agent loops" / "loop engineering" — designing systems that prompt your agents instead of you prompting them yourself.

The core definition:

  • A loop = three things: a trigger, an action, and a stop condition.
  • Loop engineering = "replacing yourself as the person who prompts the agent" — you design the system that does it instead.
  • A loop is a recursive goal: define a purpose, AI iterates until complete.
  • Two pillars that matter most: the goal (an objective, not subjective, objective) and verification (how the agent knows it hit the stop condition and how it checks/iterates).

The agent loop cycle (reason → act → observe):

  • AI reasons on what to do
  • Acts / starts implementing
  • Observes the result
  • Repeats until the stop criteria are met, then stops

The mental model he draws:

  • X-axis = attempts, Y-axis = quality.
  • AI never one-shots to 100% — attempt 1 might hit ~50%, each feedback round bumps +5-10% until you reach 90-95% "good enough."
  • The insight: outsource the feedback-and-iteration loop to the agent instead of having the human do it manually — it's going to happen either way.

Real example he built:

  • Built an agent loop to produce the HTML explainer shown in the video.
  • It checked 45 sources — articles, YouTube transcripts, X posts — looping until it had a clear idea of what to build.
  • Output wasn't V1 — it was roughly V7: it kept screenshotting, reviewing, and iterating until it decided "we're done."

His honest take (the irreverent part):

  • The opening is satire — mocking the "build a meta agent that infers what loops you'd have wanted based on your vibe" / "if you're not running 5 agents orchestrating 5 of their own agents 24/7 you're falling behind" hype (riffing on Boris Cherny / Peter Steinberg "we write loops, not prompts" tweets).
  • Reality: running swarms 24/7 without understanding what you're doing just scales problems and bugs.
  • His own usage: agents that run on a cadence or on event triggers — NOT 24/7. 24/7 only makes sense for a team constantly iterating on a product codebase.

HTTPS encrypts traffic but still leaks which sites you visit.

details

What it is: A short explainer on why HTTPS (and even encrypted DNS) doesn't hide your browsing destinations from your ISP.

How it works:

  • The padlock = HTTPS = your traffic *payload* is encrypted, but metadata isn't
  • Secure DNS (DoH/DoT) hides DNS lookups from a Wireshark capture — but doesn't solve the problem
  • The leak is SNI (Server Name Indication) in the TLS handshake, sent in cleartext, which reveals the exact hostname you're connecting to
  • A VPN fixes it by encrypting *all* traffic from the machine (not just browser) and shifting the visible endpoint to the VPN server
