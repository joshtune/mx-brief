What it is: Steve Winn (ex-Amazon Principal Engineer, ~1,000 interviews as bar raiser) live-coaches Drew, an L5 Amazon SDE chasing L6, on behavioral interview technique.

The core message:

  • 90% of smart engineers walk out of behavioral interviews with no idea what just happened
  • They tell stories that *sound* great to themselves but don't transmit the signal interviewers need
  • Three fixes: decode what's actually being asked, pick a story calibrated to your target level, package it so the interviewer can capture the signal

How to decode behavioral questions:

  • Take a list of standard questions and ask yourself: *why* are they asking this?
  • The literal question is rarely the real question
  • Practice mapping each question to the underlying trait being scored

Worked example — "Tell me about a conflict with a coworker":

  • Drew's answer: how fast you spotted conflict, actions taken, escalation, results, learnings/self-reflection
  • Steve's correction: the real question is *"are you going to be hard to work with when smart people disagree?"*
  • What they're scoring: how you frame the disagreement + whether progress was still made despite it
  • Mental model the interviewer runs: "If Drew joins our team and disagrees with someone, will he flip tables, or will he argue his case, accept being overruled, and commit fully?"
  • Learnings/self-reflection matter, but they're secondary signal

Worked example — "Time you delivered on a tight deadline":

  • Drew's answer: flagging risks early, scope thinking, asking for resources, parallelizing
  • Steve's correction: simpler than that — they're looking for *times you've delivered results*
  • Sub-signal: how big was the scope you delivered on (this is where L5 vs L6 calibration shows up)

Prep instructions for you:

  • Pull a list of standard behavioral questions
  • For each one, write down the *hidden* trait being assessed before drafting your STAR answer
  • Calibrate story scope to target level — L6 stories need bigger blast radius than L5 stories
  • Structure answers so the interviewer can literally write down the signal (frame → action → result), not so they sound good to you

What to prepare for:

  • AI can write code; behavioral is now the differentiator at senior+ levels
  • Bar raisers are pattern-matching against a rubric — your job is to make their job easy

What Interviewers Actually Look For: "Tell Me About a Tight Deadline"

1.3K

Decoding the tight-deadline interview question.

If you're interviewing soon, this tells you exactly what hiring managers are scoring when they ask the deadline question — so you can answer the actual question, not the one you think they're asking.

details

What it is: A short breakdown of the subtext behind the classic behavioral question *"Tell me about a time you delivered on a tight deadline."*

What interviewers are actually scoring:

  • How you conduct yourself under pressure — temperament and decision-making, not just the outcome
  • Track record of delivering results — concrete shipped work, not vibes
  • Scope of what you delivered — small feature vs. cross-team initiative changes the signal massively
  • Transferability — can they drop you into one of their real deadline-driven projects and trust you to land it?

The mental model they're running:

  • They have big-scope projects with hard deadlines already on the roadmap
  • They're pattern-matching your story to those scenarios
  • The question is really: *"Would hiring this person make our next deadline more likely to hit?"*

How to prepare:

  • Pick a story where the scope was non-trivial — be ready to quantify (team size, timeline, stakes)
  • Lead with the caveman version first ("Grock have rock, Grock use rock, Grock eat tonight") — clear cause → action → result
  • *Then* layer in the details: tradeoffs you made, what you cut, how you coordinated
  • Show conduct: how you communicated risk, prioritized, kept the team moving
  • Avoid hero narratives — they're hiring a teammate, not a martyr

Why it matters for you: Behavioral questions feel fluffy but they're scored on specific signals. Knowing the rubric lets you pick the right story and structure it for maximum signal — caveman-clear first, nuance second.

The featured study:

  • Source: Sweden's twin registry — hundreds of thousands of documented twins, originally tracked for health/disease research (nature vs. nurture controlled)
  • Data: Pre-2007 Swedish wealth tax forced citizens to disclose every stock, mutual fund, and dollar of savings — full financial portfolios on file
  • Method: Compared fraternal twins (50% shared DNA) vs. identical twins (100% shared DNA), ~30,000 twin pairs, plus outliers like twins separated at birth
  • Six behavioral biases measured:

  • Excess turnover (overtrading)
  • Performance chasing (buying last year's winners)
  • Home bias (overweighting domestic stocks)
  • Lottery-stock preference (loving speculative bets)
  • Disposition effect (refusing to sell losers)
  • Finding: ~45% of savings/investing behavior is genetically determined

Sam's takeaways:

  • Worldclass investing is mostly human nature, not finance — financial trends shift every decade, but human behavior is stable over millennia
