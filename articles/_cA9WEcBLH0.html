How to find your thing

Walkthrough

Framing the question

  • Shaan opens with a fan named Douglas, 24, in Austin who asked: 'They say follow your passion — but how do you actually do that?'
  • Shaan's thesis: 'follow your passion' is terrible advice. He proposes a replacement.

Why 'passion' fails

  • Most people (90%+) don't know their passion. Sam, 36, openly admits he still doesn't.
  • Without a passion to follow, people default to 'following the familiar' — what parents, friends, or peers are doing — and mistake familiarity for genuine interest.
  • Etymology: the word 'passion' comes from 'suffering' (Passion of the Christ). So 'follow your passion' literally means 'follow your suffering.'

The Joseph Campbell reframe: bliss → blisters

  • Campbell (creator of the Hero's Journey) coined 'follow your bliss,' but felt people misread it as 'do whatever feels pleasurable.'
  • He revised it to 'follow your blisters' — bliss means enduring enthusiasm, not euphoria. Criteria: naturally drawn to it, lose track of time, do it in your off-hours, others would call it work.
  • Blisters = receipts of price paid willingly. If you suffer pain willingly, that's the signal.
  • Paul Graham echo (from 'How to Do Great Work'): 'Let enthusiasm be not just the motor but the rudder of your boat.' Enthusiasm pulls you to the frontier of a field, where you start noticing gaps — and gaps are where opportunities live. Example: Sam's frontier-level interest in fitness/TRT surfaced the Hone Health investment, now 9-figure run rate.

Historical sidebar on work and leisure

  • Gilded Age (late 1800s) invented the 'man of leisure' as a class signal.
  • Before the 1930s there were no weekends or vacations. Henry Ford popularized the weekend + standard workday (originally 12 hours) after research showed it improved loyalty and productivity.
  • 1950–1970 = peak American leisure, fueled by GI Bill–funded college and post-war boom.
  • Today: we work harder than ever AND obsess over passion. Cal Newport's reframe: passion is a byproduct of mastery; mastery comes from enduring enthusiasm (Shaan's piano-before-bed example as one step toward 10,000 hours).

Shaan's tactical reframe: pick a sales motion, not an industry

  • Founders spend <15% of time on product; most time is on people, growth, and sales.
  • So pick the growth motion you actually enjoy: content, paid ads, enterprise sales, SEO, viral, influencer wooing.
  • Shaan's ranking: content (favorite) > ads > viral/sales (least favorite).
  • Sam's anecdote: after a brutal enterprise-sales meeting in NYC, he threw his 'stupid bright brown shoes' in a trash can and cabbed home barefoot, vowing never again. His least favorite: wooing influencers (Twitch streaming app days, pitching disinterested 19-year-old streamers).

The 'loop' concept

  • Every job is a repeatable loop you'll run thousands of times. Ask if you love the loop, not the title.
  • Healer loop: patient in pain → diagnose → prescribe → send back with less pain. Shaan shadowed an NFL orthopedic surgeon (his dream job) and realized the actual loop was managing chronic suffering with limited creativity — not for him.
  • Founder loop: see status quo → imagine better → build product → sell product → build team to do both.
  • Farmer loop: plant → water → grow → reap, annually for 25 years.
  • Shaan's loop (found at ~30): be curious → dig in → share top 1% with like-minded people → repeat. Six years into MFM, 'fresh as a daisy.'

Sam's counterweight: passion ≠ job

  • Sam pushes back: passion can be a hobby. Many people who turn passion into career (especially creative) struggle financially.
  • 'Braces money' anecdote: a friend who saved $30K and went sailing realized after having a kid that braces money was the new passion — got a steady job.
  • Shaan partially agrees but argues most people work ~half their waking adult life, so finding a job (not necessarily entrepreneurship) you actually like is worth fighting for. Cites 70% of people don't like how they spend their days.

Bronnie Ware's Top Five Regrets of the Dying

  • Hospice nurse, thousands of dying patients. Observational, not scientific. The five:

1. I wish I had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected (by a huge margin)

2. I wish I hadn't worked so hard (mostly men, missed family events)

3. I wish I had the courage to express my feelings

4. I wish I had stayed in touch with my friends

5. I wish I had let myself be happier

Two practical takeaways

1. Name the blisters upfront. Before committing, list the specific daily costs (early mornings, gym on bad days, watching what you eat). The blisters decide whether you'll like it more than the rewards do.

2. Practice the art of noticing — your own disproportionate enthusiasm, or someone else's observation of it. Naval's mom told him he'd be a businessman (he wanted to be a scientist) because of how he critiqued every pizza shop. Adam Neumann's wife Rebecca told him to do real estate because his eyes always went up to the buildings in NYC.

Key takeaways

  • Don't follow your passion — follow your blisters: evidence of suffering you willingly pay for is a stronger signal than fleeting interest.
  • Pick the sales/growth motion, not the industry. You'll spend <15% of your time on product; the loop you'll actually run is the growth motion.
  • Every job decomposes into a repeatable loop. Ask whether you love the loop itself, not the title or outcome.
  • Mastery comes from enduring enthusiasm — the only force that gets you to 10,000 hours. Passion follows mastery, not the reverse.
  • Name the blisters upfront before committing. The daily costs determine fit more than the rewards do.
  • Practice the 'art of noticing' — your own disproportionate enthusiasm, or what observant people in your life see in you that you can't.

Gotchas & caveats

  • Passion doesn't have to be your career — Sam warns many creative passions don't pay. 'Braces money' from a steady job can be the right answer.
  • If you do try entrepreneurship, save 6–12 months of expenses first. Lack of money will reliably make you unhappy even if money won't reliably make you happy.
  • External comparison kills internal scorecards instantly (the $18M penthouse browsing example). Build an internal scorecard or you'll keep moving the goalposts.
  • Bronnie Ware's regrets list is observational, not scientific — treat it as wisdom, not data.
  • Cliché advice ('follow your enthusiasm,' 'be true to yourself') sounds easy but is incredibly hard to apply day-to-day — it's the pole on the airport tram, not a magic answer.
