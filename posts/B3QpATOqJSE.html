Why Your Brain Is Holding You Back

Codie Sanchez · 2026-07-31 · 11,004 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Loss aversion is quietly blocking your first bet.

It names the exact cognitive tax that keeps you researching side hustles instead of starting one — and gives three concrete levers to reprice the bet.

What it is: A 90-second breakdown of Kahneman's prospect theory and how loss aversion gets in the way of every wealth-building bet you don't take.

The research:

  • Daniel Kahneman's prospect theory (the Nobel work — note: Codie says "Nobel Peace Prize"; it was actually the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences)
  • Study setup: a 50/50 gamble — 50% chance to win $100, 50% chance to lose $100
  • Result: most people won't take the bet until the upside is 2–3x the downside. They need $200-to-win vs. $100-to-lose before they'll play
  • Diagnosis: losses hurt more than equivalent wins feel good. But money is made on the other side of bets, so the wiring is actively expensive

How to fix it — three moves:

  • Flip the fear. Stop pricing what you lose if it fails. Price what you're already losing by staying put. Inaction has a cost line too; most people just never write it down
  • Increase the potential reward. Don't argue with your brain's 2–3x demand — satisfy it. Engineer the payoff so the upside genuinely is multiples of the downside, then the bet clears your own internal hurdle rate
  • Increase your odds. Turn the 50/50 into a 70/30, then a 90/10. Two accelerants: (1) surround yourself with winners, (2) take a lot of reps

The engineering translation:

  • This is asymmetric bet sizing. Capped downside, uncapped upside — the same reason a weekend side project beats quitting your job cold
  • "Increase your odds" = reduce variance through information and iteration. Reps are just sampling; proximity to people who've done it is a cheap prior
  • The 2–3x hurdle explains why you keep passing on decent opportunities that *are* positive expected value. Your brain isn't running EV, it's running a loss-weighted distortion of it
