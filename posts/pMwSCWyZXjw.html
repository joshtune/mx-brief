The pricing lesson every founder should learn

My First Million · 2026-07-29 · 3,123 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
1775 steam engine startup invented outcome-based pricing.

It's a 250-year-old case study in charging for outcomes instead of units — the exact pricing move that lets a product sell itself into the accounts where it's worth the most.

What it is: A short clip breaking down how Boulton & Watt priced the steam engine in 1775 — and why it's still the smartest pricing model in business.

The featured business: Boulton & Watt, the partnership between engineer James Watt and manufacturer Matthew Boulton, selling an improved (far more coal-efficient) steam engine into British mines.

How they made money — the actual mechanics:

  • Zero upfront price. They gave the engine away free. No capex hurdle, no procurement fight, no "let me think about it."
  • Charged a third of the coal savings. Customer paid a recurring cut — one-third — of the money the new engine saved them on coal versus their old inefficient one.
  • Sold to installed base, not greenfield. They went to mines *already running* inefficient steam engines. The comparison baseline already existed, so savings were measurable and undeniable from day one.
  • Hardware as a service, 200 years early. The engine was a delivery mechanism for a metered savings contract, not a product sale.
  • Perfect incentive alignment. They only made money when the customer made money. No selling shelfware.
  • Geographic arbitrage on customer value. Coal cost more the farther a mine sat from a coalfield — remote tin mines paid the most. Those customers saved the most, so Boulton & Watt earned the most. The pricing model automatically routed early installs to the highest-value accounts.
  • Captured the upside instead of a flat fee. A fixed sale price would have left the entire savings surplus with the customer. The percentage take scaled with the value created.

The lineage:

  • Rolls-Royce "Power by the Hour" — airlines pay per hour the jet engine is in service, not per engine bought. Same model, ~175 years later.
  • Modern equivalents: usage-based SaaS, outcome-based AI pricing, performance-fee agencies.

The transferable playbook:

  • Find customers already paying a measurable, recurring cost.
  • Remove the purchase decision entirely (free to install).
  • Price as a percentage of the savings or revenue you generate.
  • Let the model self-select into the accounts where your value is highest.

Why it matters for you: If you ship anything that measurably reduces a cost or lifts a number, you can skip the pricing-page guessing game and just take a cut of the delta — the customers who benefit most will find you.
