Why You Should Ask for a Raise (And How to Do It)

A Life Engineered · 2026-08-02 · 2,040 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
The math favors asking for a raise.

Most engineers never ask, and the ones who do win about 70% of the time — this is the two-minute script for the conversation.

What it is: A short, tactical breakdown of the raise conversation — the odds, the opening line, and the framing that gets your manager on your side instead of across the table.

The numbers he cites:

  • Only 30% of people ask for a raise; 70% never do
  • Of the 30% who ask, 70% get something — maybe not the number they wanted, but something
  • Net: asking is a positive-expected-value move. Silence guarantees zero.

How to ask:

  • Don't open with "I want a raise." It's personal and it's selfish. What the company hears is: *"I'd like more money for the same work."* From the other side of the table, that's straightforwardly bad for the bottom line.
  • Give reasons. You need to hand your manager an argument they can carry upward.
  • *Reason 1 — performance:* "Look at what I've done." Concrete, delivered, measurable.
  • *Reason 2 — humanize it:* find a framing that makes you a person, not a line item. The most common one, if it's actually true for you, is family.
  • Enlist your manager. The whole game is getting them to *want* this to happen. Not "you need to give me a raise" — instead: *"I'd like to make more money in order to better take care of my family. How can we work together so that that's an obvious case?"*
  • Make it collaborative. You're not making a demand, you're opening a joint project where the goal is making your raise the obvious call.

The script, condensed:

  • ❌ "I want a raise."
  • ✅ "I'd like to make more money in order to [reason]. How can we work together so that becomes an obvious case?"

What to prepare:

  • A written record of what you've shipped and its impact — the performance half doesn't work from memory
  • An honest, true humanizing reason (don't manufacture one; it's transparent and it burns trust)
  • A concrete ask, so "work together" has a target to aim at
  • Willingness to accept a partial win — "something" is the modal outcome, not the full number
