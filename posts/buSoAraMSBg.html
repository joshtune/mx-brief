How De Beers manufactured diamond demand from scratch

A case study of how De Beers turned a common rock into a 'rare' luxury by controlling supply and engineering demand.

What it is: Sam Parr's case study of De Beers and the diamond industry, framed as a marketing masterclass. The transcript provided cuts off early, so this covers the setup and the supply-side play, not the full demand-side campaign.

The business / featured entity:

  • De Beers — formed from the consolidation of Cecil Rhodes' mining interests and a partner's. It became the dominant diamond supplier.
  • Starting problem: diamonds were historically rare and worn only by royalty as a signal of power and wealth. They had nothing to do with love or proposals.
  • Trigger event: a huge discovery of South African deposits in the late 1800s turned rare into plentiful. Sam compares it to scooping diamonds 'like Baskin Robbins', a threat to anyone whose pricing depended on scarcity.

How they made money (what was covered):

  • Consolidated supply: bought up the mines. Took about 20 years from the discovery to lock up the supply.
  • Manufactured scarcity: with supply controlled, they could hold prices high even though the stones were no longer naturally rare.
  • Set up the demand play: the hosts call it a 'natural experiment'. Diamond engagement rings were NOT the norm (under 10% of people bought one) and are now the majority practice in the Western world. That gap is the marketing story.
  • Teased but not in this excerpt: the pitch that men should spend about 20% of their income on a ring (from the episode description). Also the positioning of natural vs. lab-grown vs. 'blood' diamonds.

Current market (the hook):

  • Diamond prices are down about 70% from peak, and about 50% since COVID.
  • A 2-carat lab-grown diamond now costs about $148, 'the price of dinner'.
  • Parr's wife still wants the ring anyway, which is the proof the marketing works.
