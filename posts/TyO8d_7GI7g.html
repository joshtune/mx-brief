The Only 4 things that actually sell (pick one)

My First Million · 2026-08-04 · 21,688 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Seven sales rules from a legendary Hacker News comment.

A 20-year sales veteran's seven-point framework compressed into something an engineer can actually act on — plus Sam Parr's documented experiment using reciprocity to knock $400 off a motorcycle.

What it is: Shaan Puri reads out what he calls the highest-value comment he's ever seen on Hacker News — seven sales rules written by a guy with 20 years of leading sales orgs, aimed squarely at engineers who say "I suck at sales." Sam Parr then breaks down Cialdini's reciprocity rule and a real experiment he ran with it.

The 7 rules (verbatim substance):

  • 1. Sales is like golf. You can make it so complicated it's impossible, or you can walk up and hit the ball. Advice: walk up and hit the ball.
  • 2. It's about people and problem-solving. Not tech, not solutions, not chemicals, not lines of code, not artichokes. People. Problems.
  • 3. People buy four things and four things only, ever: *time, money, sex, and approval/peace of mind.* Sell anything else and you fail.
  • 4. People buy aspirin, always. They buy vitamins sometimes, and unpredictably. Sell aspirin.
  • 5. All things being equal, people buy from friends. So make everything else equal, then go make a lot of friends.
  • 6. Being valuable and useful is all you'll ever need. Send interesting posts, write birthday cards, record videos with ideas for growing their business, make intros between people who'd benefit — then get out of the way and expect nothing back. Do it consistently and authentically and people find ways to give you money.
  • 7. Nobody cares about your quota, payroll, opex, or burn rate. They care about the problem you solve for them. "There are more than a hundred trillion dollars in the economy just waiting for you to breathe it in."

The reciprocity play (Sam's $400 case study):

  • Source: Robert Cialdini's *Influence* — the rule of reciprocity. Do someone a small favor and they're disproportionately likely to return a much larger one. The two asks don't need to be remotely equal.
  • Sam's setup: age 24, motorcycle listed at $1,800. He documented the whole thing with photos as he went and wrote it up as a blog post in 2014.
  • The move: showed up late, apologized, said he'd stopped for a Coke because it was hot — and bought one for the seller too. Handed it over before any negotiation.
  • The ask: "I love this bike, but I'm a young guy, I don't have a lot of money. I know you're asking 18 — could you do 14?"
  • The result: "You brought me the Coke. You seem like a nice kid." $1,400. ~$400 saved on a roughly $1 Coke.
