$9B a year from subscriptions

My First Million · 2026-08-17 · 15,939 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Cintas turned uniform laundry into a $75B subscription machine.

A 97-year-old rag-cleaning business quietly compounded into $9B/year of recurring revenue by selling boring, contract-locked services no one wants to think about — the purest case study in subscription economics you'll see this week.

What it is: The origin story of Cintas — from cleaning circus performers' rags in 1929 to a $75B market cap company doing ~$9B/year in subscription revenue.

The business, start to finish:

  • 1929 — Founder (Richard "Doc" Farmer) starts collecting and cleaning discarded rags and uniforms from circus performers. Names it Acme Industrial Laundry.
  • Stays small for three decades — a local, low-margin laundry operation.
  • 1960 — The son enters the business with a growth mandate. Revenue at the time: $180,000/year (~$2M in today's dollars).
  • ~1968 — After 8 years, revenue hits $1.6M/year (~$15M today). Roughly 9x in 8 years, ~31% CAGR.
  • The unlock: stop cleaning rags, start laundering company uniforms — recurring, contracted, B2B.
  • Rebrands to Cintas (from *uniformes cintas*).
  • Today — ~$9B/year revenue, ~$75B market cap.

How they actually make money:

  • Uniform rental + laundering — they don't just clean the uniforms, they *supply* them. Customer never owns the asset, so they can never stop paying.
  • Route density — the truck is already going to that restaurant every week. Every additional SKU on that truck is near-pure margin.
  • Attach-rate expansion — once the route exists, they bolt on:
  • Fire extinguishers and fire protection services (that extinguisher in the restaurant bathroom? Cintas.)
  • First aid and safety cabinets
  • Cleaning supplies, mats, mops, restroom supplies
  • Document management
  • Multi-year contracts — revenue is subscription in the strictest sense: recurring, auto-renewing, with switching costs baked in (uniforms are branded and sized to *their* inventory).
  • Consolidation — decades of acquiring regional laundries to buy route density rather than build it.

Why the model is so durable:

  • Nobody's boss ever got fired for the uniform vendor. Low-consideration purchase = low churn.
  • The service is physical and local — no software competitor can commoditize a truck route.
  • Cost per customer falls as density rises; competitors entering a mature market face structurally worse unit economics.
  • Price increases pass through quietly because the line item is small relative to a customer's P&L.
