The $4 billion business behind drug testing

My First Million · 2026-08-12 · 16,628 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Charles River Labs monopolizes preclinical drug testing on animals.

A $4B/year monopoly hiding in plain sight — one company sits between 80% of biotech and the FDA, and the input cost (monkeys) 10x'd from a geopolitical supply shock.

What it is: A short segment on Charles River Laboratories, the testing-as-a-service company that runs animal trials for most of the pharma industry, plus the non-human primate (NHP) supply crisis that sent monkey prices from $2K to $50K.

The business — Charles River Labs:

  • Model: testing-as-a-service for biotech. They don't invent drugs, they run the required preclinical animal trials.
  • Owns the animals and the facilities: mice, rats, dogs, and monkeys, all in-house.
  • Works with roughly 80% of biotech companies; ~80% of drugs that reach market were tested through them.
  • ~$4 billion/year in revenue. Described on the pod as "an absolute monopoly."
  • The moat is regulatory + logistical: FDA requires animal testing before human trials, and almost nobody else can breed, house, and run studies on primates at scale.

The monkey supply shock (the actual money story):

  • The US imports roughly 20,000 monkeys per year; tens of thousands more move globally.
  • Pre-COVID price: ~$2,000 per monkey.
  • COVID hit → every vaccine and therapeutic program needed primate testing at once → demand spike.
  • China supplied ~60% of the world's NHPs and went cold turkey on exports, keeping them for domestic use.
  • Result: prices ran to $20,000–$50,000 per monkey — a 10–25x move on a live input.
  • Cambodia and Vietnam stepped in as replacement suppliers, which is where the supply chain sits now.

The pattern worth stealing:

  • The most durable businesses are often the mandatory middleman — a step every player in an industry is legally required to pass through.
  • Watch for single-country input concentration (China at 60% of NHPs). One policy decision re-priced an entire industry's cost base overnight.
  • Biological inputs can't be scaled on demand — breeding cycles are years long, so supply shocks last years, not quarters.
