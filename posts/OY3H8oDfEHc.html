Magic: The Gathering is Hasbro's $2B cash cow.

A 30-year-old card game compounds 17% annually off one mechanic-level insight — the clearest case study you'll find in how a single design decision creates a permanent revenue engine.

What it is: Shaan breaks down Magic: The Gathering — the 1993 card game that now drives ~$2B/year and roughly a third of all Hasbro revenue.

The featured business — Magic: The Gathering (Wizards of the Coast, owned by Hasbro):

  • Created by Richard Garfield, a mathematician who was tinkering on games while working a day job
  • Launched 1993; now ~$2B annual revenue, ~$500M in a single recent quarter
  • Grew 17% a year on average for 17 straight consecutive years — not a fad curve, a compounding curve
  • 1 of every 3 dollars Hasbro takes in is driven by Magic. Hasbro also owns Monopoly and Nerf — Magic outweighs both
  • Hasbro stock popped on the news that Magic hit an all-time high quarter

How they actually make the money:

  • Collectible card packs, not a boxed game. The core mechanic: players bring their *own* collection to the duel instead of using pieces that ship inside one box. Garfield lifted this from marbles
  • That single insight converts a one-time $40 purchase into an unlimited, recurring spend — every player is a permanent customer, every new set is a new SKU they must buy
  • Collecting > playing. Like Pokémon, a large share of demand is people who never duel — they buy for the collection, scarcity, and secondary-market value
  • Endless new set releases keep the treadmill running; old IP never fizzles because the product is the *supply of new cards*, not the rules
  • Two-player duel format keeps the social/competitive loop alive, which drives demand for better cards, which drives more pack sales

The origin story (the part worth stealing):

  • Garfield first built a game called Five Magics — went nowhere, but he poured real work into it
  • Years later, on a hike, the eureka: take Five Magics and swap in the marbles mechanic (bring your own collection)
  • Lesson Shaan hammers: the power of tinkering. The failed project wasn't wasted — it was the raw material the billion-dollar idea got applied to
