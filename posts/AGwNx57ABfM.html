$1.5B VC: “Founders need a savage mindset”

My First Million · 2026-11-16 · 24,619 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Speed is the only startup advantage that beats incumbents.

James Currier (4x CEO, $1.5B VC) says the one trait that predicts founder success is speed, and he shows how Mike Cassidy went from startup to a $500M sale in 500 days.

What it is: Shaan Puri (episode 882) interviews James Currier, who teaches a Stanford startup class. They cover his "speed talk": of the 48 founder traits he looks for, they all roll up to how fast a founder moves. *Note: the transcript I had cuts off early, so this covers the opening of the episode only.*

The featured person: James Currier

  • Was CEO of four venture-backed companies before becoming an investor.
  • Sold Tickle for over $100M (per Shaan's intro).
  • Co-founded NFX, a venture firm that has raised about $1.5B.
  • Spent 25 years in startups and built a 48-item checklist of founder traits, then reduced it to a single metric: speed.
  • As a CEO, his biggest problem was making things happen fast, because most things you try don't work.

How he made money:

  • Founder exits: He built and sold venture-backed companies, including the 100M+ Tickle sale.
  • Venture investing: He launched NFX and now invests that ~$1.5B in startups. His edge is pattern-matching on founder speed.
  • Teaching: He runs a Stanford class that spreads his framework and feeds his deal-flow reputation. This is my inference, not something he said.
  • *The excerpt doesn't give deal sizes, returns, or fund economics beyond the figures above.*

The core argument:

  • More resources won't beat a better player. In chess, an extra rook doesn't help you against a grandmaster.
  • Moving twice for every one of their moves does beat a grandmaster.
  • Startups face the same situation against bigger, better-funded incumbents. Speed is the one lever a small team always has.
  • Star Wars analogy: Luke has only a tiny X-wing and speed. Biggs doesn't call a meeting, he says "I'm right with you." Your team has to match your pace.

Case study: Mike Cassidy, $500M exit in 500 days

  • Cassidy was a 1990s entrepreneur in Boston who ignored the normal speed bar. The excerpt doesn't name the company.
  • Prototype: 3 weeks instead of 12 months.
  • Fundraising: 3 days instead of 3 months.
  • Hiring: 72 hours instead of 6 months. He interviewed candidates and had the offer ready to sign at the interview.
  • VC process: He lined up every VC and met them all within 36 hours, which framed it as a competition to invest. That pushed the VCs to move fast too.
  • Office: He pre-selected a lease or sublease so space was ready immediately.
  • Currier met Cassidy in the late 90s and realized this was the future of startup speed.
