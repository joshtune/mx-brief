Rory Sutherland on psychology beating product economics

Rory Sutherland breaks down the exact reframing moves — inventing units, pricing on saved cost, turning flaws into features — that let ordinary products win without any change to the product itself.

Who he is: Rory Sutherland — Vice Chairman of Ogilvy UK, author of *Alchemy*, career built on behavioral-economics-driven advertising. Recently went viral after someone uploaded clips of his old talks, and he leaned into it.

The core thesis:

  • "There are two ways of making money. You can either make desirable things, or you can make things desirable."
  • Physics is fixed. Psychology is "magnificently malleable" — so perceived-value engineering has a far better ROI than incremental product engineering.
  • An invention isn't an innovation until it changes behavior. (He credits Slack's Stewart Butterfield with the same framing.) You can build anything; if nobody adopts it, it's a lab curiosity.
  • Corollary he keeps hammering: the "trivial" details delegated to junior staff are often the whole ballgame. Small arbitrary changes → monumental effects.

Case study: how James Watt invented horsepower (a marketing unit)

  • Watt & Boulton were selling steam engines to mine owners, whose actual job was draining flooded mines so miners could work.
  • Engineers pitched boiler calorific capacity and piston stroke length. Buyers did not care.
  • They asked instead: what does the buyer want to know before writing the check? Answer: *how many horses can I stop feeding?*
  • So they invented a unit — horsepower. Note that unlike the Watt, Newton, Coulomb, or Celsius, it's not named after a scientist. It exists purely to make the buying math obvious.
  • The pitch became: a 25hp engine runs 24/7, so it replaces ~75 horses working in shifts.
  • Mine owners then did the math themselves — cost of horses, feed, upkeep — on the back of an envelope, and ordered two. The buyer sells himself when the unit is right.

Case study: hardware-as-a-service in 1775

  • Competitors had already-installed, less-efficient Newcomen engines. Rip-and-replace is a hard sell.
  • Watt & Boulton's pricing: *free engine, you pay us one third of the coal savings.*
  • Zero capex, payment indexed to realized value, and it only costs the buyer if it works. Modern outcome-based SaaS pricing, 250 years early.
  • Rolls-Royce later ran the same play with jet engines — airlines pay per engine-hour rather than buying the engine ("Power by the Hour").

The transferable playbook:

  • Invent the unit of measurement your buyer thinks in, don't educate them into yours.
  • Price against the cost you eliminate, not the thing you deliver.
  • Take a stated product disadvantage and re-narrate it as the strength — Sutherland calls this alchemy: literally turning lead into gold.
  • Treat the industrial revolution as a marketing revolution: abundant supply is worthless without manufactured demand.
