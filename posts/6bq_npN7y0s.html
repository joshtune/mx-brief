Lloyd Blankfein's Retirement Portfolio Got Him Roasted Live

My First Million · 2026-06-10 · 7,494 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Ex-Goldman CEO day-trades his billions; advisor roasts him.

A masterclass in what NOT to do with serious wealth — straight from the guy who ran Goldman Sachs getting publicly schooled on risk-adjusted returns.

What it is: A clip where wealth manager Barry Ritholtz roasts retired Goldman Sachs CEO Lloyd Blankfein for actively day-trading the bulk of his net worth.

The featured individual — Lloyd Blankfein:

  • Former CEO of Goldman Sachs (one of the most powerful jobs in finance)
  • Now retired, and self-admittedly addicted to day trading
  • Says he's *always* grabbing his phone to check stocks — got so anxious about a 2-hour podcast that he pre-placed all his trade orders in advance
  • Claims roughly 70% of his net worth (~a couple of billion dollars) is actively traded
  • Says he's "doing good" on returns
  • Post-podcast routine: market's closed, nothing else to do, so he walks 2 miles home

The roast (Barry Ritholtz's take):

  • Calls actively trading 70% of net worth "the biggest risk-adjusted mistake of your career"
  • His prescription: that 70% should sit in municipal (MUN) bonds paying a large tax-free yield
  • Carve-out: keep a few million dollars to actively trade for fun — "knock yourself out" — but don't gamble the core
  • The kicker: "the schmuck that used to run Goldman Sachs should know better"
  • Joked he's now uninvited from Shabbat dinner for the burn

How they (would) make money — the actual lesson:

  • For large, already-won wealth, the move is capital preservation + tax-efficient yield, not active trading
  • Municipal bonds: interest is typically federal- (and often state-) tax-free, so the effective yield on a high earner's money is outsized
  • Risk-adjusted return > raw return: minimizing downside on billions beats chasing alpha you don't need

Why it matters for you: A blunt reminder that even elite operators fall for active-trading dopamine — and that the boring, tax-optimized allocation is usually the smarter play once you've already made your money.
