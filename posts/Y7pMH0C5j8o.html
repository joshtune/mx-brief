The secret behind horsepower

My First Million · 2026-07-27 · 18,309 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Horsepower was invented as a marketing unit, not science.

A 90-second lesson in why James Watt out-sold every other steam engine maker — he stopped pitching specs and invented a metric that priced his product in the customer's own currency.

What it is: A short clip arguing that invention ≠ innovation, illustrated by how James Watt and Matthew Boulton invented "horsepower" purely as a sales device.

The featured individuals:

  • James Watt — Scottish engineer, improved the steam engine (separate condenser, 1769 patent)
  • Matthew Boulton — his business partner; Boulton & Watt was the commercial vehicle, not Watt alone
  • The pairing is the point: engineer + commercializer. Watt built it, Boulton sold it, and the *unit* was the joint invention

The core thesis:

  • "An invention isn't an innovation until it changes behavior."
  • If nobody adopts it, you built an invention — not an innovation
  • Historians file Edison, Ford, Watt, Boulton under "inventors." The clip's argument: their real edge was adoption engineering

How they actually made the money:

  • Target customer: mine owners, who used teams of horses to pump water out of mines
  • The failing pitch: engineers talked boiler calorific capacity, piston stroke length — spec language the buyer didn't parse and didn't care about
  • The reframe: Boulton & Watt asked "what do these people actually need to know before they'll buy?"
  • The answer: *how many horses do I stop feeding if I buy this?* — an ongoing operating cost the buyer already tracked in his head
  • The invention: "horsepower" — a unit that converts an engineering spec into the customer's existing cost model
  • The tell: it isn't named after a scientist (unlike the ohm, the newton, the watt). It's named after the thing being replaced. That's a marketing unit, not a physics unit
  • The commercial payoff: the pitch becomes arithmetic the buyer does himself — X horses × feed cost = savings. Boulton & Watt famously priced engines on a share of the fuel/cost savings they delivered

The transferable pattern:

  • Denominate your product in the unit the customer *already pays in* — headcount, hours, horses, servers, support tickets
  • Never make the buyer translate your spec into their cost model. Do the translation for them and ship it as the unit
  • Whoever defines the measurement standard defines the comparison — and every competitor now has to sell on your axis
