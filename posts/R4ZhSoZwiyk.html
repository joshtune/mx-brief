I Failed At Being Visible, Until I Understood This

A Life Engineered · 2026-10-12 · 16,596 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Visibility starts with notable, measurable work, not volume.

If you're trying to get noticed for promotion, this says to stop shouting and fix the work first: pick metrics people care about and plan how you'll measure impact before you start.

What it is: A former Amazon principal engineer argues that visibility is a byproduct of notable work, not self-promotion. He says to fix the work before you amplify it.

The message to engineers:

  • Standard advice (be louder, do more) is backwards. Loud mediocre work just makes mediocrity visible.
  • Step one is notable work, meaning it has meaningful impact or makes you an outlier.
  • Photography analogy: a young National Geographic photographer's shots were technically perfect, but an editor told him to "get in front of more interesting shit." Flawless execution on unremarkable subject matter is still unremarkable.

What to prepare for:

  • Unmeasured work is invisible work. If you can't measure it, you may not get credit, even if it was objectively impactful. He admits that's unfair.
  • Hard-to-measure areas like developer experience and productivity are the classic traps. You can put in huge effort and get no recognition.
  • Counting activity isn't impact. Early on he tracked interviews conducted, but nobody cared. They cared how many good people were hired, and that wasn't being measured.
  • Metrics are local. What counts depends on your company: revenue, cost savings and profit margin, features shipped, adoption, monthly active users.

Instructional checklist:

  • Find out which metrics your org and leadership actually care about. If nobody cares about the metric, the impact isn't meaningful.
  • Before starting a project, ask: "How will I measure the impact?"
  • Do a bit of high-leverage measurement work up front, such as instrumentation or a baseline.
  • Prefer quantitative metrics. Qualitative ones are acceptable if that's all you can get.
  • Money is the easiest metric to get noticed with (more revenue, lower cost).
  • Ask whether your work would survive into a promotion packet. A refilled coffee pot wouldn't.

Case study (Amazon):

  • The Just Do It Award goes to engineers who show bias for action and have big impact. The CEO gives you a Nike shoe on stage.
  • A junior engineer on the search results team noticed a missing string on the German product page (the "only a few left in stock" text).
  • The obvious fix was to add the string to the localization database. Instead, he set up an A/B test on the page so the impact would be measurable.
  • The fix saved Amazon millions per year, needed no code, and got him promoted immediately.
  • The transcript cuts off mid-story, so the exact A/B test details aren't covered here. The lesson is clear: measure it so it counts.
