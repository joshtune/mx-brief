AI Software Factories Are the Next Big Thing (And I'm Building You One)

Cole Medin · 2026-09-03 · 36,657 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Cole commits his channel to an open-source AI software factory.

This is Cole announcing the direction his entire harness-engineering output will follow for the next year — PRD-in, deployed-code-out with zero human code review — which is the ceiling your mx-workflow pipeline is currently walking toward one command at a time.

What it is: A manifesto + retrospective, not a tutorial. Cole declares his next big project: an open-source "AI software factory" (his "dark factory") you can clone and point at a PRD, and reports on the year-long experiment that convinced him it's viable.

The factory loop he's describing:

  • PRD / high-level planning doc goes in — the only human artifact
  • System decomposes it into individual tasks/tickets
  • Agents build each task, open PRs, review those PRs, merge
  • Deploys straight to production — no human reads the code
  • Human steers direction only: "we still drive the vehicle with higher-level directions, but there's no steering wheel anymore"

What he's doing differently now:

  • Betting the channel on it. Previous stance: level 3 is where reliability lives, and that's what he teaches. New stance: level 5 is productive *today*, at minimum for prototyping and spiking product ideas — and he's building the open-source reference implementation rather than just demoing it.
  • Removing himself as the error filter, deliberately. His stated reason is the sharpest idea in the video: if he can't catch the agent's small mistakes, he's forced to make the harness reliable enough that they don't happen. Autonomy as a forcing function for harness quality, not as a convenience.
  • Proof by artifact, not by demo. DynaChat — an agentic AI-tutor chat app with access to his full YouTube catalog and the Dynamis community content — was built end-to-end by the factory. He has never read a line of its source. That's the evidence he's leading with.
  • Framing his taxonomy explicitly around Dan Shapiro's five levels of AI coding autonomy (the driving-a-vehicle analogy: L1 you write the code with light assist → L3 agent writes everything but you own planning and validation per ticket → L5 console with no steering wheel). He says he'll keep referencing it — it's becoming his shared vocabulary.
  • Thesis on why now: harness, model, and workflow layers are all improving *in parallel*. Reliability isn't one breakthrough away; it's three compounding curves. He's also careful to disclaim the maximalist read — not "AI replaces engineering in a year," but "factory becomes a standard tool in the belt within a few years."

How mx-workflow can improve from this:

  • You already have the factory's parts — they're just human-triggered. `/mx:prd` → `/mx:plan` → `/mx:implement` → `/mx:qa` → `/mx:pr` → `/mx:shipit` is Cole's loop with a human keystroke between every stage. The gap isn't capability, it's the connective tissue: one entry point that takes a PRD and runs the chain without returning to you between stages.
  • `/mx:build` is your closest thing to the factory door. It already chains discovery → PRD → plan → build → QA → commit → ship. Worth deciding explicitly: is that the factory, and everything else is a manual escape hatch? Name it that way if so.
  • Your review agents are the differentiator, not the builder agents. Cole's honest bottleneck is that nobody reads the code. You have `mx-code-reviewer`, `mx-silent-failure-hunter`, `mx-performance-auditor`, `mx-type-design-analyzer`, and `mx-quality-keeper` — an adversarial review bench most factory attempts lack. `/mx:reject` emitting a machine-readable verdict is exactly the primitive an unattended loop needs to halt on. That's your edge: a factory that can *refuse to ship*.
  • `/mx:ratchet` is the missing safety rail most people won't build. "Quality only goes up" as a gate on an autonomous loop means the factory can't silently degrade the codebase across dozens of unattended merges. Wire it as a hard stop in any autonomous chain, not an optional check.
  • `build-with-agent-team` is your parallel task-decomposition story. Cole's factory splits a PRD into tasks and builds them concurrently — you already have tmux-split agent teams with a mandatory QA teammate that routes failures back to owning agents. That's further along than the video implies most people are.
  • Concrete next move: define the autonomous exit criteria. What makes the loop stop and page you — `/mx:reject` verdict, ratchet regression, N failed QA rounds on one task? Cole's insight is that removing yourself forces the harness to get good; you can only remove yourself once the halt conditions are explicit.

Why it matters for you: This is the roadmap episode for where Cole's agent-skill teaching is heading, and it reframes mx-workflow's job — you don't need more commands, you need the autonomous spine that runs the ones you have and the halt conditions that make running them unattended safe.
