How the Ferrero family built a $20B chocolate empire.

A three-generation case study in how one product tweak (and obsessive secrecy about the machines that make it) compounds into a $32B fortune.

What it is: A short breakdown of the Ferrero family — Italy's richest, worth ~$32B — and the chocolate empire behind Nutella, Ferrero Rocher, Tic Tacs, and Kinder.

The people & the business:

  • Ferrero family, Italy — richest family/individual in Italy at ~$32B net worth.
  • Company does roughly $20B/year in revenue.
  • Brand portfolio invented in-house: Nutella, Ferrero Rocher, Tic Tac, Kinder bars, Kinder eggs.

How they made the money — step by step:

  • Gen 1 (grandfather, post-WWII): Italy is economically wrecked, chocolate is an expensive luxury good. He starts making chocolate anyway — and instead of competing on solid bars, he creates a creamy chocolate spread in a jar called Supercrema. Cheaper per serving, stretches scarce cocoa, sells into a market that can't afford luxury chocolate.
  • Result: Supercrema does okay — a real business, not a breakout.
  • Gen 2 (the son): More of an operator/innovator than his father. He renames Supercrema to Nutella and tweaks the recipe to make it better. Brand + product improvement turns a decent regional product into a global category-owner.
  • Product line extension via lab work: The son gets into the lab and notices breath mints are a good product with real problems (form factor, packaging, portability). He invents the Tic Tac form factor — the flip-top plastic case is arguably the product, not the mint.
  • Vertical integration as moat: Ferrero builds its own production machinery rather than buying it. Proprietary machines = competitors can't replicate the texture, the shell, the fill.
  • Extreme secrecy as strategy: Factories are locked down. A journalist who got inside described it as "less like Willy Wonka, more like Area 51" — armed guards on site, because the machinery itself is the trade secret.

The pattern worth stealing:

  • Enter an expensive category with a cheaper form factor (jar spread vs. luxury bar).
  • Second generation wins on branding + recipe iteration, not on the original invention.
  • Own your manufacturing — the machine is the moat when the recipe alone can be reverse-engineered.
  • Line-extend by fixing the packaging/form factor of an existing commodity (mints → Tic Tacs).
