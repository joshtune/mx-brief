The Visibility Habit That Got Me To Principal Engineer

A Life Engineered · 2026-10-30 · 11,921 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Make your work visible or stay stuck at senior.

If you're a strong senior engineer who isn't getting promoted, this explains why depth isn't the bottleneck and gives you a concrete daily habit to fix it.

What it is: A pitch for proactive self-reporting as the unlock for promotion past senior, told through the author's 4-month daily-email habit on a ~100-team, billions-at-stake project.

How it works:

  • The message: Past senior, promotion isn't about technical depth. It's about which projects you're on, who knows your work, and how your story lands in rooms you're not in.
  • The reframe: You're not choosing between humble and self-promoter. You're choosing between invisible and seen.
  • His example: A TPM already sent formal status reports. He sent his own daily email to thousands of people, junior engineers through VPs. It covered progress, what help was needed, blockers, and what was next.
  • Why it worked: People who didn't care filtered it out. Everyone else got a daily reminder of who was driving. At launch nobody had to be told what his role was.
  • His cost: He was stuck at senior for 8 years before working this out.

What to prepare for:

  • Promotion past senior is judged on scope, sponsorship and narrative, not on being the deepest engineer on the team.
  • Someone with less technical depth but more visibility can beat you for the promotion.
  • Your work will be summarized by other people in rooms you're not in. If you haven't given them the story, they'll tell a worse one.
  • Visibility at this level takes sustained effort over months, not a single announcement.

Instructional steps:

  • The game: Never make your manager ask for your status. If they have to ask, you lose. It also frees your 1:1s from status updates.
  • If broadcasting feels gross, start with other people:
  • Call out a teammate's contribution in a public channel.
  • Recognize someone's good work in a meeting.
  • Message their manager, e.g. "Hey, I wanted you to know Raj really came through on this project."
  • It takes about 30 seconds and costs nothing. Nobody finds it annoying.
  • Then include yourself, factually: "The team shipped this feature on time. Raj's testing was crucial and I'm proud of the integration work I contributed." That's accurate reporting, not bragging.
  • Do it today: Open your brag book, pick the latest win, and share it in a team channel or status update. Start with someone else's win if you need to.
  • For big projects: Consider a regular broadcast (daily or weekly) covering progress, blockers, asks and next steps. Recipients can filter it out if they don't want it.
