The next pickleball is already making $40M/year

My First Million · 2026-11-18 · 49,705 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Oh My Mongg: mahjong's $40M aesthetic-tile boom

A three-year-old mahjong brand went from $1M to $40M+ by turning a $20 board game into a $450+ lifestyle object, and the hosts walk through the demand signals behind it.

What it is: Oh My Mahjong, a company selling beautifully designed mahjong sets, grew from about $1M in year one to $40M in year two and is on pace for roughly $60M in year three. (The transcript excerpt cuts off before the full breakdown.)

How it works (what they did to make money):

  • Premiumized a commodity. A mahjong set is a $20-30 board game. The brand sells design-forward tile sets as aesthetic objects. The 'entry-level' set at a Bay Area shop was $450, and the host's wife and he left having spent about $850 on a beginner kit.
  • Sold the full kit. The $850 was the set plus the accessories 'you need to play', so each customer bought several items instead of one.
  • Rode a social trend. Mahjong is the 'next pickleball': a social, low-athletic activity for older players, and a remix of something familiar. The founder is described as a woman who started a company 'making beautiful tiles'.
  • Had a distribution channel. Retail shops carry the sets and sell them in person. Playing happens in groups, so each buyer pulls in friends.
  • Growth trajectory: Year 1 about $1M, year 2 about $40M, and year 3 (about 2.5 years in) has already passed $40M, with a guess of about $60M by year end.

Demand signals cited:

  • Yelp searches for mahjong club, coach, and related terms are up about 4,000%, and Yelp named it a top trend.
  • Google search volume is up about 70% year over year, and has climbed every year since COVID.
  • Mahjong lessons are a side business: a Greenwich, CT instructor reportedly made about $200/hr teaching wealthy retirees.
  • Mahjong social events are spreading. An 8am post-drop-off 'mahjong plus tennis' birthday party in the East Bay had 20-30 moms, nearly all semi-pro players.
  • The game is hard to learn, which creates demand for lessons, clubs, and coaches.
