I Had Fable 5.1 and 5 Build Me the Same App

Nate Herk | AI Automation · 2026-09-03 · 53,977 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Fable 5.1 vs Fable 5 building the identical app

It's a real orchestration-prompt teardown — the exact delegation prompt Nate used, plus the cost and runtime deltas ($1,200 vs $500, 1.5 days vs half a day) you'd actually be budgeting for.

What it is: A head-to-head bake-off — same prompt, same spec, two models (Fable 5.1 and Fable 5), each acting as an orchestrator that delegates all execution to sub-agent workers.

The setup:

  • Identical prompt to both agents, word for word. The *only* diff: "you are Fable 5.1" vs "you are Fable 5", plus the working folder.
  • Neither model was allowed to build the app itself. The prompt scoped the top-level agent to "strategy, planning, delegation, sequencing, quality standards, and final acceptance" and explicitly forbade it from doing engineering research, debugging, testing, or visual design production.
  • Model routing was specified in the prompt: Opus workers for architecture, product/design direction, hard problems, and independent reviews; Sonnet workers for implementation, research, testing, debugging, iteration.
  • Anti-drift clauses: give workers clear ownership, prevent conflicting edits, replace or redirect workers when results are weak, "worker failure is still your responsibility."
  • Definition of done was hard-coded: don't stop at a plausible implementation — keep orchestrating until there's objective evidence the product works and is presentation-ready.

The build target — "Ops Flow":

  • Local-first visual automation studio for designing and *simulating* incident response workflows.
  • Explicitly not an n8n-style execution platform. It's a rehearsal tool: draw your emergency plan as a flowchart, then watch it play out.
  • Target user: the person who owns what happens when production breaks.

The results (the headline numbers):

  • One build cost ~$1,200, the other ~$500.
  • One took a day and a half, the other half a day.
  • "It wasn't even close" — the two apps look and feel materially different despite the identical spec.
  • Nate withholds which is which during the walkthrough so you form your own opinion first.

Build A (unattributed in the clip):

  • Left-rail palette: trigger, condition, action, approval, resolution — drag onto canvas, wire together.
  • Vertical node graph rather than the usual horizontal flow.
  • Run button drives a live visual execution: nodes light up as they process, ends in a run summary (e.g. 7 executed, 3 skipped on a branch, resolution "mitigated by rollback").
  • Rough edges Nate hits live: run panel won't drag-expand to full screen, no scroll-to-zoom on the canvas — you have to navigate manually.
