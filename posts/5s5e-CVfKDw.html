How to Get So Much Done It Feels Like Cheating

A Life Engineered · 2026-11-13 · 1,145 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Four habits for getting promoted by doing less

AI is pushing output expectations through the roof (2,000 PRs a month at SpaceX), and this is a playbook for winning through leverage and judgment instead of grinding harder.

What it is: A former Amazon Principal Engineer's argument that fast promotions come from cutting effort smartly, not from shipping more. The transcript is truncated, so only the first two of four habits are covered below.

How it works:

  • The message: AI-fueled output is becoming the baseline (a SpaceX principal reportedly shipped 2,000 PRs in a month), so volume won't set you apart. The people promoted fastest, who often look the laziest, are the ones who master a different set of behaviors.
  • Principle 1: Find the minimum acceptable effort.
  • Projects get worse as you add to them: more pieces means more complexity, which means further from done.
  • Still do everything that's actually required, and skip the extras.
  • Amazon example: during the Prime Video Q4 code freeze, he was assigned to review every change across an org of thousands of engineers. Instead of working nights and weekends, he set two rules his manager approved.
  • Rule 1: the default answer to any freeze change is no, unless the VP approves it.
  • Rule 2: he only reviews changes backed by a comprehensive change-management plan, pre-approved by the principal engineer closest to the change.
  • Result: his workload dropped to roughly zero, and he still got credit for a holiday season with no code-caused availability incidents.
  • Past junior level nobody hands you the minimum requirements, so you set the effort level yourself. Most people just accept whatever they're handed.
  • Prime Video example: ops teams loved Excel macros more than the complex validation systems engineers kept building. Dev cost was essentially zero.
  • Principle 2: Don't automate it, just don't do it.
  • The engineer's instinct is to script or AI-automate every mechanical process.
  • His story: he spent days automating a dashboard check in a ticket SOP, then discovered the dashboard already had monitoring. The SOP predated that monitoring and was never removed.
  • Automation creates work: you have to specify it precisely, verify it, and then inherit the bugs.
  • All code is a liability, AI-generated included. Automate it and you own it forever.
  • The transcript cuts off mid-sentence here, and principles 3 and 4 are not included.

Do this (instructional checklist):

  • Before any big project, write down what's essential to hit the target outcome. Do the simplest thing that clears the quality bar.
  • When you're handed a gatekeeping or review role, make the default answer "no." Require pre-attached sponsor approval and a written plan before you spend any time.
  • Before building a new system, ask whether Excel or a macro can do the job.
  • Before automating a recurring task, ask whether it still needs doing at all. Check whether the SOP is obsolete or something else already covers it.
  • Get your manager to sign off on your reduced-effort rules. That covers you if something breaks.

What to prepare for:

  • Raw output volume is turning into a commodity because of AI. Your edge is judgment about what not to build.
  • Every line of AI-generated code is code you maintain. Expect to be evaluated on outcomes (no incidents, things shipped), not on activity.
