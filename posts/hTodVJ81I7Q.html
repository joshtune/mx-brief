The powerful family behind everything you eat (and why they hide)

My First Million · 2026-07-23 · 29,360 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
How the Cargill family built America's biggest private company.

A masterclass in why owning the physical middleman position — not cutting it out — became one of the largest fortunes in America.

What it is: A "Billy of the Week" breakdown of Cargill — the largest private company in the US for 40+ years, 88% family-owned, and the single biggest producer of billionaires in one family.

The business (how they made the money):

  • Started as a grain middleman. As America built out railroads, farmers needed to offload crops. Instead of selling individually and arranging their own transport, they used Cargill.
  • Owned the physical chokepoint. Cargill built grain elevators (giant storage silos) directly next to the railroads — the one spot everyone had to pass through to ship grain. Nobody else was going to build that infrastructure.
  • Two models for farmers: (1) drop off grain, get an IOU ("coat check") and get paid when it sells, or (2) sell it outright at a flat wholesale rate so Cargill could flip it.
  • Vertically integrated relentlessly. Shipping barges were inefficient, so they built their own shipyard and ships — eventually producing vessels for the US Navy.
  • Expanded into protein. Bought one of the biggest poultry/meat companies, taking control of a huge share of the food supply chain.

The core business lesson:

  • The received wisdom is "cut out the middleman." Cargill is the counterexample — the middleman became worth more than everyone else in the chain.
  • Why it worked here: the middleman was *physically* in the middle. They owned the irreplaceable infrastructure (elevators + rail adjacency + ships), so the position couldn't be disintermediated.

Scale:

  • More revenue than Goldman Sachs, Nike, and Starbucks combined.
  • 88% owned by the Cargill/MacMillan family.
