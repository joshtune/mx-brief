6 Signs You Sound Junior (And Don’t Even Know It)

Speaking habits that make engineers sound junior in meetings

If you keep hearing 'speak up more,' this gives you two concrete swaps (drop the apologies, lead with questions) that change how leadership reads you.

What it is: A former Amazon Principal Engineer (20 years, thousands of meetings) lists the speaking habits that read as junior. The transcript is cut off after mistake #2, so signs 3-6 aren't covered here. The title says six and the description says five.

The message to engineers:

  • Your technical skill isn't the bottleneck. How you open your mouth is.
  • 'Speak up more' feedback is common, but speaking up badly is worse than staying quiet.
  • Someone else will say your point more confidently and get the credit.

Mistake 1: Apologizing before you speak

  • The pattern: Preamble like 'sorry, this might be dumb, but...' negates you before you've said anything.
  • Why we do it: We want to speak up but not look arrogant, so we shrink.
  • Why it backfires: Softeners give the room permission to ignore you.
  • Instead: Cut the preamble and say the thing. You don't need a power pose, just be comfortable.
  • Fixing the arrogance worry: Stop trying to sound smart. Apologizing doesn't fix it.

Mistake 2: Lecturing to show how smart you are

  • The story: In a payments design review, Principal Engineer Peter Voshall had the speaker whiteboard 'how we don't lose transactions if the primary DB host is unreachable.' Then he asked 'where's the queue hosted?' It was on the same host as the DB. He knew the flaw all along.
  • Why it worked: The speaker internalized the lesson instead of just ticking a checklist item, and walked out impressed by Peter's depth.
  • Key idea: Every question carries a premise. A good question demonstrates your depth and probes theirs.
  • Why lecturing fails: You don't know what the room already knows, and in a big company someone there likely knows it as deeply as you.
  • Junior vs senior: Juniors impress with knowledge. Seniors ask the question that cuts through.

Instructional: how to build a premise-loaded question

  • 1. Start with the conclusion you want to announce to the room.
  • 2. Ask what single fact got you there.
  • 3. Ask about that fact instead of announcing the conclusion.
  • Example (cut off mid-sentence): Instead of 'the launch date is a fantasy,' ask about the security review, which hasn't started and takes weeks.

What to prepare for:

  • Design reviews and big meetings where your phrasing gets judged as seniority signal.
  • Feedback like 'speak up more' or 'be more confident.' Treat it as a delivery problem, not a knowledge problem.
  • Before your next meeting, write your main point as a question built on one key fact.
