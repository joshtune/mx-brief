Corporate politics 101 for engineers who hate politics.

The core message: "work hard and hope to be noticed" is the strategy that makes you interchangeable with everyone else in the room — Evans gives you the replacement.

What it is: Interview with Ethan Evans — ex-Amazon VP (Prime Video, Twitch, 800+ person orgs), now retired and financially unmuzzled — on why hard work alone never gets you promoted, and how to actually play corporate politics.

The core message to engineers:

  • Hard work is table stakes, not a differentiator. It's what makes you the *same* as everyone else at a place where everyone works hard.
  • "My work will speak for itself" is the single belief Evans would rip out of engineers' heads.
  • Most people's entire career strategy is *work hard and hope to be noticed*. That's not a strategy — it's a lottery ticket.

Politics, redefined:

  • Politics ≠ dirty. It's just the word for human interaction, relationships, and negotiation.
  • Working definition: how businesses and the people in them reconcile competing goals against limited resources.
  • Concrete shape of it: you and a peer both have products to ship, you have dependencies on each other, you each want your own schedule first. Resolving that *is* politics.
  • Career version: several engineers all want to grow. Someone gets the marquee project, the CEO presentation, the key business trip. Someone doesn't. Pretending that allocation is purely meritocratic limits you.

Evans' rules (what's covered in the transcript):

  • Rule 1 — Accept that politics are real. Drop the "I shouldn't have to care about this" stance. Evans hates the word *should* because it disempowers you — you end up waiting for what *should* happen instead of doing what you need to do. Relationships consciously and subconsciously color how you're treated.
  • Rule 2 — Be reasonable. The worst thing Evans can say about someone is that they're unreasonable. Unreasonable = illogical (the engineer's reading) and intransigent, stuck on a viewpoint, close-minded. That second half is the one engineers miss.

Other threads teased in the episode:

  • Spotting manipulation before it costs you — "there's a lot of nasty things you can do."
  • When your manager is the problem: "you can suffer or leave. So pick one and get started."
  • Asking for money — the one hard stat in the video: only 30% of people ask for raises. Of that 30%, 70% get something. The 70% who never ask get nothing by definition.
  • Lying at work: Evans opens with "every manager and every employee lies. Yes, I lie. I hate it. And I don't think it's avoidable."

What to prepare for:

  • Expect visibility to be allocated, not earned passively. If you want the high-leverage project, ask for it before the decision is made.
  • Expect your reputation for *reasonableness* to be evaluated as heavily as your technical output — especially on cross-team dependency negotiations.
  • If you haven't asked for a raise, you're in the 70% who statistically get nothing.
