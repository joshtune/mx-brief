5000 Hours of Building AI in Just 17 Minutes

Nate Herk | AI Automation · 2026-08-04 · 41,085 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Nate's hard-won lessons from 5,000 hours building AI.

No build-along here — it's Nate's career/positioning playbook, useful mainly for the signal that he's moved off n8n to Claude Code and why he thinks tool choice is the least durable part of the skillset.

What it is: A talking-head retrospective — no demo, no repo, no workflow JSON. Nate distills 5,000 hours of AI building (seven-figure agency, 400k+ people taught) into a numbered lesson list.

The lessons (transcript covers 1–4 before cutting off):

  • 1 — Portfolios are dead, collect receipts. Two viable paths into AI: start an agency, or become "the AI person" internally. Nate tried the internal route at Goldman Sachs, judged the red tape too slow, and left. His point: everyone now has the same portfolio built from the same tutorials, so buyers can't tell a real operator from a weekend demo. Fix = document outcomes, not builds. *"This process took X hours, now takes Y. We were missing N leads, now we catch all of them."* Write the number down, record a quick walkthrough — even on a personal project. Three real outcomes beats five workflow screenshots. He's building a certification program at AI Automation Society specifically to close this trust gap.
  • 2 — Tools don't matter (said by the n8n guy). Notable admission: his main tool is now Claude Code, not n8n. The argument is that the transferable asset was never the tool — it was knowing what an API call is, where things break, and how to read and fix an error. All of that carried straight over from n8n to Claude Code. Same for artifacts: an "AI operating system" is just folders, markdown files, and instructions, so it runs on Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, or whatever ships next month. If yours *doesn't* transfer, that's a design smell — build tool-agnostic.
  • 3 — AI-native is a default, not a knowledge base. Not how many models you can name — what you reach for first when work lands on your desk. And "can AI do this?" is never binary; the real question is *to what extent*. 70% coverage is a huge win, 25% is still a win over doing it fully manually. Re-ask periodically: the models are the worst they'll ever be right now.
  • 4 — The AI isn't the valuable part. Transcript cuts mid-lesson, but the thesis is stated: thinking the best prompts/model/skills win is backwards, because the AI is the one thing everyone has. (Implied follow-on: distribution, domain access, and client relationships are the moat.)
