He built a $2B+ business at 77

How Grey Goose's founder invented premium vodka from nothing

Sidney Frank built a $2B+ liquor brand in his 70s using pure positioning — no product innovation, just a made-up origin story, a taller bottle, and a price 30% above the market leader.

What it is: Shaan and Sam retell the Sidney Frank playbook — the guy who made Jägermeister a US party staple, then launched Grey Goose in his 70s and sold it to Bacardi for ~$2B.

Who he is:

  • Sidney Frank, liquor marketer, started Grey Goose in his 70s (transcript says "in his 70s"; the title pins it at 77)
  • Prior act: imported Jägermeister to the US and turned a German digestif into a college party drink
  • Exit: Grey Goose sold to Bacardi in 2004 for roughly $2B+ — one of the largest spirits deals ever

What he did to make money — Jägermeister:

  • Took a bitter, unloved German liqueur nobody was drinking in the US
  • Invented the ritual: created the Jäger bomb, giving the product a use case instead of a flavor pitch
  • Built a field sales army — "Jägerettes," girls sent into bars to pour shots directly for college kids
  • Bought distribution at the point of consumption rather than paying for mass advertising

What he did to make money — Grey Goose (the actual playbook):

  • Faked the terroir. Team said "we're flying to Moscow" for the vodka. He said no: *go to France.* They told him France has no vodka. His answer — "go to France and find me vodka, because if I told you this is French vodka, it just makes that dirty Russian vodka look like crap." Country-of-origin as positioning, not sourcing logic.
  • Priced off the competition, not the cost. Sent a team to a bar to buy every expensive vodka on the shelf, lined the bottles up, and set Grey Goose at 30% above the most expensive one. Price as the quality signal.
  • Changed the silhouette. The category standard was a fat bottle. He made it tall — instantly identifiable on a backbar from across the room.
  • Net: no distillery breakthrough, no new flavor. He manufactured a premium category and then owned it.

The transferable pattern:

  • Product is a commodity; the story, the price anchor, and the shelf presence are the moat
  • Pick your reference class deliberately — he wasn't competing with vodka, he was competing with "expensive French thing"
  • Distribution beats advertising when the buying decision happens in a specific physical moment (the bar)
  • Age is not a constraint on the founder side; category knowledge compounds
