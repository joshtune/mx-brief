How to Progress Faster Than Everyone Else (as an engineer)

A Life Engineered · 2026-10-23 · 31,524 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Six promotion principles from a 4x-promoted Amazon Principal Engineer

A former Amazon bar raiser tells you to stop polishing effort and start producing evidence and next-level behavior, and both habits are things you can start this week.

What it is: A former Amazon Principal Engineer (4 promotions, ~1,000 bar raiser interviews) lays out six principles for faster career progression. The transcript cuts off after principle two, so only those two are covered here.

The message to engineers:

  • Talent and hard work aren't enough. Excelling at your current level only proves you're good at your current level.
  • Promotions stall on *how hard you worked*. They sail through on *undeniable evidence of impact*.
  • You don't need anyone's permission to act like the next level.

Principle 1: Being promotable requires evidence

  • Leave artifacts your manager can point at to prove you're operating above your level.
  • Example: A junior engineer won Amazon's Just Do It Award (CEO recognition plus a Nike shoe from Bezos) and got an immediate promotion. The task was tiny: add a missing "Buy now, only 3 left in stock" string to search results.
  • The real move: Before doing the work, he asked "how will I know the impact?" and set up an A/B test in about an hour. The string was worth about $2M in annual sales.
  • Instructional takeaways:
  • Before starting any big piece of work, ask: *what graph goes up and to the right because of this?*
  • Set up measurement before you build, not after. Don't do great work and then try to gather evidence retroactively.
  • If the impact is unmeasurable or only indirectly measurable, rethink whether the project is worth doing as currently framed.
  • You don't need million-dollar fixes, just clear, measurable results.

Principle 2: Don't wait for permission to behave like the next level

  • It's duck typing for careers: if you act like a senior, you get treated like a senior.
  • Example: He was blocked on someone outside his team, a level above him, and hesitated to bother him. His manager said to just tell him it's needed by Monday. He sent a long, apologetic email and got a one-word reply: "Okay."
  • There's no floating level tag over your head. At Meta it's hard to even tell people's levels, and work still gets done.
  • Real permission gates are few: interviewing for senior roles, or approving expenses without being a manager.
  • Exercise:
  • Take your current work and ask how someone one level up, and then two levels up, would do the same work.
  • Almost none of the differences require permission. Do those things.

What to prepare for:

  • Your manager will need evidence for a promo packet. Start collecting it now.
  • Build the habit of defining success metrics in the design or kickoff phase, before code.
  • Expect to feel awkward cold-pinging people above you. Do it anyway, and be brief and direct.
