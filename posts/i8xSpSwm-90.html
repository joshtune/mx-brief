5 Things World Class Software Engineers Do That You Don’t

A Life Engineered · 2026-07-27 · 14,258 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Copyable habits that separate good engineers from great

Steve's message: the gap between you and the engineers pulling ahead isn't talent — it's a handful of habits you can start running this month, and he hands you the exact self-check questions to run.

What it is: Steve (ex-Amazon principal, ~1,000 bar-raiser interviews, host of a podcast with principal/distinguished engineers) distills the repeating behavioral patterns he sees in top-fraction-of-a-percent engineers at Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Google. Five habits, plus a teased sixth that "may be the only one that matters in 5 years" given how fast the industry is shifting.

The habits (as covered in the available transcript):

1. Measure growth in months, not years

  • From Kun Chen (interviewed on his podcast): *"I think people wait until they are not getting promoted to realize, oh, I'm not growing. That's a very lagging indicator."*
  • The test: "What did I do this month that I couldn't do last month?"
  • Waiting on a promotion cycle to find out you've stalled costs you a year minimum.
  • Two failure modes it fixes: the slow drift (one meaningful skill per year — far too slow) and the shiny-object churn (new framework drops, new AI tool drops, you chase both, learning constantly but shallow and scattered).
  • Note the verb — "what can I do?" not "what do I know?" Knowledge expires. A capability you can put to work doesn't.

2. Be an amplifier, not an oracle

  • Michael Navody: intern → principal at Meta in 6 years by being "the coding machine" — whole codebase in his head, shipping at a volume nobody could match. Then he hit the wall: *"There's only so much one person can do themselves. The coding machine is a lone wolf kind of thing."*
  • Meta's feedback at the next level wasn't *code more* — it was broaden your influence. He organized a company-wide code health summit, pulled a dozen people doing hard migration work into one room. That's what earned the promotion.
  • Amazon's version of the anti-pattern: the senior engineer whose approval every design needs, who every decision routes through. Feels indispensable; is actually the team's biggest bottleneck. Internal phrase: "Don't install yourself as an oracle."
  • Be an improver, not an approver. Leveling up the people around you is a rare win-win-win: they get better, the company gets a stronger employee, you get credit for scaling past your own two hands.

3. Solve the right problem — introduced via Christopher Brown (distinguished engineer); the transcript cuts off here. Habits 3–5 and the tie-it-together sixth are in the full video.

Instructional — do this today:

  • At the start of each month, write one sentence: *"By the end of this month, I'll be able to ______."*
  • Fill it with a capability, not a topic. "Learn Kubernetes" fails the test. "Deploy an application," "diagnose a production issue," "design a system I couldn't design before" pass.
  • 12 months = 12 concrete, pointable-at proofs of growth — exactly the evidence a promo packet needs.
  • Audit yourself for oracle behavior: if decisions queue on your approval, convert one of those queues into a doc, a summit, or a person you've trained up.
