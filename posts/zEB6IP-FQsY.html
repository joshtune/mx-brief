Carlyle co-founder on building $500B from $5M

Rubenstein lays out the exact sequence from unemployable Carter aide at 31 to founding Carlyle at 37 and scaling it to $500B AUM, plus the $14B Bezos deal he passed on.

What it is: Sam Parr interviews David Rubenstein, co-founder of The Carlyle Group, about his path from blue-collar Baltimore to White House aide to private-equity billionaire, and the lessons that made the money.

Who he is:

  • Son of blue-collar Baltimore parents who never finished high school
  • Deputy domestic policy adviser in the Carter White House at 27, despite admitting he wasn't qualified
  • Co-founded Carlyle in 1987 at age 37 with $5M from four investors
  • Carlyle now manages roughly $500B

How he made money (the sequence):

  • Got proximity to power early. Worked ~1 year on Capitol Hill, joined the Carter campaign, won, and landed a White House job at 27. His edge: he compiled every campaign promise by hand (no internet), so he was the only person in the room who could say "that violates a promise." Unique knowledge got him a seat in the president's meetings.
  • Learned that power is rented. When Carter lost, the people who'd promised to hire him stopped returning calls. No law firm wanted a junior Carter aide. He calls himself "unemployable" at 31. Lesson: relationships built on your position evaporate with the position.
  • Started late, on purpose. At 37 he reasoned "if I don't do it now, I'll never do it" and raised $5M from four investors to found Carlyle in 1987.
  • Scaled through fundraising and deal-making. The DC-based firm leveraged political and government connections into a private-equity, buyout, and asset-management platform that grew from $5M to $500B AUM over ~four decades.
  • Never got comfortable. Even at year 10 he didn't feel safe; he still expects something bad to happen every day. He frames this as the entrepreneur's default state, noting 99.9% of US startups aren't in business 5 years later.

The $14B miss:

  • Met Jeff Bezos before Amazon took off; Bezos offered 20% of the company he was about to build
  • Rubenstein passed. He later admitted "we should have taken that deal, that was stupid"
  • That stake would be worth ~$14B today

Key takeaways:

  • Founders who win have "staggering" self-confidence; the paranoia never leaves
  • Become the person with the information nobody else bothered to compile
  • Your network built on a job title is not your network
  • Late starts work: 37 is not too old to build a $500B firm
