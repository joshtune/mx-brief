3 low competition businesses minting MILLIONS right now

My First Million · 2026-08-19 · 20,041 views  ·  Watch on YouTube →
Founders pitch boring businesses doing eight figures.

Nick Haschka has run the same playbook three times — buy an unsexy service company nobody thinks about, bolt on acquisitions, and end up with two platforms doing $22M combined.

What it is: Episode 853, "Shoot Your Shot" — MFM's format where founders get 15 minutes on stage in San Francisco, lead with their single juiciest number, pitch, then ask Sam and Shaan anything. First up: Nick Haschka of OnPoint Generators.

The featured operator — Nick Haschka:

  • Background: management consulting → corporate innovation/strategy at NRG (large US power company)
  • Not a founder-from-scratch type — self-described "management consultant nerd, ivory tower, here's your board deck"
  • Had one failed venture-backed startup before switching strategies entirely
  • Post-failure thesis: "There's got to be an easier way to make money sitting on my couch cruising BizBuySell the way a lot of people do with Zillow"

Business #1 — the office plant company (2017):

  • Bought it in 2017 as his first "search" acquisition
  • Yes, literally interior plants for offices
  • 14 bolt-on acquisitions on that single platform since
  • ~$10M revenue, a little over $2M SDE/EBITDA
  • Still owns it with a partner

Business #2 — OnPoint Generators (the current pitch):

  • The big number: $12M ARR, line of sight to $14M this year, contracted out to ~$25M
  • Backup generator sales and service in California
  • Third search venture; started with a thesis, then hunted for a service provider to buy
  • Bought company #1 in January 2024, company #2 about 5–6 months later — roll-up from day one

Why generators (the origin insight):

  • At NRG doing innovation projects, backup generators were one area where they *could not find good expertise*
  • That expertise gap = the market inefficiency he bought into
  • Product: if power drops for 1 minute or 12 hours (storm, earthquake), the unit kicks on
  • Customers are non-optional buyers: every hospital, surgery center, nursing home, telecom tower, police station, fire station, data center — plus high-end estate properties buying industrial-grade units
  • Mostly B2B; higher-end units, not consumer Home Depot stuff

Industry structure (the part worth stealing):

  • "Very barbelled" — giants at one end (Caterpillar, Cummins, Generac) competing for marquee names, big county and state contracts; a long tail of mom-and-pops at the other
  • Lion's share of the market sits with the big platforms, which is exactly what leaves the fragmented middle acquirable

The repeatable playbook:

  • Work in an industry, notice where expertise is scarce
  • Form a thesis *before* searching, not after
  • Buy an existing operator instead of starting cold (search fund model)
  • Immediately acquire a second one to prove the roll-up mechanic
  • Compete against giants on service and against mom-and-pops on capability
  • Repeat the pattern across unrelated boring industries — plants and generators have nothing in common except fragmentation

Shaan's framing: "There are all these businesses that as soon as somebody says them, I'm like, *ah, of course* — yet they've been a complete blind spot to me my entire life."
